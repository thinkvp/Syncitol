How to use Syncitol
✕
How it works

Syncitol reads when each clip was recorded, lays every clip out on a new -SYNC sequence so the gaps between recordings match real clock time, then lines the audio up by waveform — first whole tracks (coarse), then every clip (fine). Your original sequence is never changed.

No external install is needed: the FFmpeg audio decoder ships inside the plugin.

⚡ Running a sync

Make your original sequence active (not an already-built -SYNC — Auto Sync refuses those) and click Auto Sync. It runs four steps — reading clips, building, coarse align, fine align — and the busy row shows which one, the elapsed time, and once a step has run long enough to tell, about how long that step has left (the audio search is uneven, so treat it as a guide). ✕ Cancel stops it at any point.

When it finishes, the log’s Result line says how it went: how many clips matched, how many are worth checking, and how many sat outside every reference recording. Clips worth checking get a “Check sync” marker on the timeline at their start, with the reason in the marker’s comment. The Sync Results table lists each track and clip’s shift, what it matched against, and a confidence score.

↩ Revert (in Sync Results) undoes the shifts the sync applied. It is not available when the tracks were packed afterwards (see Track layout) — run Auto Sync again from the original sequence instead.

The three options

All three default to Auto, which is right for almost every shoot. After a run, each one left on Auto shows what it decided.

Audio reference

The track every other clip is aligned to. Auto picks the one with the most recorded coverage. Choose your field recorder or main camera’s sound to force it. The list shows audio tracks only: a camera clip’s picture and sound are one file, so V1 and A1 would pick the same recordings. A forced track holding every clip leaves nothing to align, so the panel falls back to Auto and says so.

Track layout

Everything after the build treats one track as one recording device. If a track holds two — every clip dumped on V1, a recorder sharing a camera’s audio track — the -SYNC sequence is built fresh with one block of tracks per camera and recorder. Auto-detect does this only when a track provably mixes devices; Keep my tracks never does; Rebuild by device always does.

Only things one device cannot do prove a mix-up: different containers (.MXF and .MP4), camera clips beside sound-only recordings, two camera serials (from Sony M01.XML sidecars), or two files recorded at the same moment. Folder, bin and file name keep files apart but never trigger a rebuild on their own — one camera’s two cards on V1 is fine. A rebuild places every file whole, so trims, effects and titles from the original timeline are not carried over.

After a rebuild, once the sync is done, device blocks that never overlap in time are packed onto shared tracks — the same camera on two days, or two devices that never rolled together. That means placing them again in a fresh copy, which only replaces the first one if every clip lands exactly. A forced Audio reference follows its recordings through a rebuild.

Recording days

Laid out by the clock, day 2 would land a whole night after day 1. Syncitol finds the recording sessions — footage with no break longer than two hours — and closes the gaps between them. Within a day nothing moves, and each day gets its own audio reference, so clips are only compared with recordings from the same day. A sequence marker names each day. Auto-detect does this only when the recording clocks span more than 24 hours and at least one device recorded on more than one of those days; Single day never does; Multiple days always does.

Clocks months apart with no device in common are one camera with the wrong date, not two shoot days — Auto lays that out as one day and says so. Files dated only by when they were copied or processed never count as a day; they are searched against every day. After the sync each day slides, as one piece, to start a minute after the previous one ends. A timeline still longer than 24 hours is built anyway — Premiere accepts it, but its timecode display rolls over at 24:00:00, so use the day markers.

Recording times and clocks

Each clip’s start comes from its embedded creation time, Sony’s modification date, or timecode. A file with none falls back to its file date minus its duration — tagged mtime in the Detected Clips table. Keep file dates when copying media, or that fallback is wrong.

Tracks that were recording at the same time vouch for each other’s clocks and are laid out at their real offsets, leaving the audio pass a fine-tune instead of a hunt. A camera whose clock is off by a whole number of hours or days (time zone, daylight saving, a date reset) is laid out where its clips line up with the others at that offset, provided several start within a couple of minutes of theirs. Anything else nothing corroborates starts at 0:00 and the audio pass finds its place.

Consistent timestamps are not automatically real ones. A bulk download, a batch transcode or a camera with no clock stamps every file alike. Syncitol checks what none of them can fake: one device cannot record two files at once. A track whose own clips claim to overlap is excluded from the clock layout and flagged.

How the alignment works

The coarse pass moves each whole track to fix minute-scale clock offsets. A clip over 90s is probed at points across its whole length and the offset must hold at more than one; it is then re-tested on the track’s other clips, and if they agree on something else, theirs wins.

The fine pass then places every clip against the reference within ±5s, re-searching ±30s for clips it cannot match there; only corrections over 20 ms are applied. Overlaps over 2 min are matched at both ends, and the two must agree.

One camera carries one clock error, so its clips’ corrections cluster. A clip asking for more than 1.5s away from the rest of its track keeps that only on a strong match; otherwise it gets the track’s typical shift and a Check sync marker. Clips the fine pass could not match get their track-mates’ shared correction when they agree closely.

Clock drift — two devices whose clocks run at slightly different rates — is flagged only when it reaches a frame (40 ms) across an overlap of 10 minutes or more; anything smaller is invisible and within measurement error. One offset can’t fix both ends of a drifting clip; split long recordings if it matters.

Every clip of one file moves as one piece, so a camera’s picture and sound never come apart. Audio linked from a different file (merged clips, Synchronize, Clip > Link) moves with its picture. After every move the timeline is read back; anything that landed unevenly is put right, or back where it started.

Speed and the cache

When Premiere’s own peak files (.pek) cover your media, whole tracks are matched from them in milliseconds without decoding any audio. Right after an import, or when a project opens in a new Premiere version, Premiere is still building them, so the first sync is slower — the log says when that is the case. Audio Syncitol does decode is cached on disk, so a re-run on the same footage takes seconds. The log reports what each step actually decoded.

To time a sync as a first run, clear Syncitol’s cache: the decoded audio, the peak-file index and the file metadata it has read. Premiere’s own peak files are Premiere’s — clear those under Preferences › Media Cache if you want to time a run without them. Windows and network drives also keep recently read files in memory for a while, so a second run straight away can still be quicker.

Reporting a problem

The Log is the full record of a run. ⬇ Export .txt writes it to a file — send that with a bug report.

Updates

Syncitol asks GitHub once a day whether a newer release exists and shows a banner if so — dismissing it hides that version, not the next one. Click the version number in the footer to check now. Nothing else is ever sent or received.
